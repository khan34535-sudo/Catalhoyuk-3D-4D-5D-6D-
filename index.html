Field Monograph

Çatalhöyük: Archaeological Synthesis & Bioarchaeological Insights

Health, Diet, Stature, Architecture, and Communal Living (7100–5700 BCE)

Çatalhöyük represents a remarkable 1,400-year sequence of dense, streetless agglomerated living. With an estimated population numbering between 3,000 and 8,000 individuals dwelling in mudbrick structures sharing common party walls, the site offers deep lessons regarding early human urbanization (Hodder, 2006).

1. Pathologies, Smoke Inhalation & Health

Examinations of over 700 human skeletons reveal the physiological impacts of high-density Neolithic life (Larsen et al., 2015):

  • Carbon & Soot Deposits: Embedded soot layers in rib bones and chronic sinus inflammation reflect heavy exposure to indoor particulate smoke from roof-level hearths and ovens.
  • Infectious Stress Indicators: Elevated frequencies of cribra orbitalia and skeletal periostitis point toward chronic nutritional stress and parasitic loads linked to animal proximity and organic waste middens.
  • Interpersonal Conflict: Approximately 25% of crania exhibit healed depression fractures from small round clay projectiles, suggesting ritualized or internal dispute resolution without lethal outcomes.

2. Physical Labor & Biomechanics

Femoral cross-sections indicate tremendous daily mobility. Inhabitants traversed surrounding marshlands and alluvial fields to harvest timber, clay, obsidian, and wild grains. Arthritic wear in lumbar vertebrae and knees demonstrates countless hours spent kneeling on plaster floors while grinding grain (Larsen et al., 2015).

3. Culinary Technology & Subsistence

Residue analysis on unbaked clay spheres and ceramic vessels highlights a rich agrarian and foraging diet (Hendy et al., 2018):

  • Cereals & Pulses: Emmer wheat, einkorn, barley, lentils, and bitter vetch consumed daily as porridge or gruel.
  • Animal Fats: Wild aurochs tallow, sheep/goat fats, and emerging evidence for early dairy processing.
  • Indirect Stone Boiling: Clay spheres heated in embers and placed into liquid-filled baskets or wooden vessels for cooking prior to the widespread use of direct-heat cooking pots.

4. Lithic Technology & Obsidian

Obsidian was a prized imported material, and the site's obsidian caches show how it was stored, worked and used (Carter, 2007, 2011; Carter & Milić, 2013; Carter & Shackley, 2007):

  • Sourcing: Geochemical (EDXRF) sourcing links the site's obsidian to Cappadocian volcanoes such as Göllü Dağ (Carter & Shackley, 2007).
  • Hoarding: Obsidian blanks were cached in sub-floor hoards, as in the Building 1 pit (Carter, 2007).
  • Polished Mirrors: Polished obsidian pieces in the Building 52 sequence were reflective objects, not just cutting tools (Carter, 2011).
  • Projectile Technology: Bifacial hunting points from the Building 77 cache document late-phase projectile production (Carter & Milić, 2013).
